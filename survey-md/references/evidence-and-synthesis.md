# Evidence And Synthesis

Use this reference when building sources, paper notes, evidence matrices, critique, or cross-paper arguments.

## 1. Evidence Standard

Tie every substantive claim to evidence appropriate for its strength. Separate:

- bibliographic facts;
- authors' stated methods, results, and limitations;
- comparisons supported by multiple sources;
- survey-level inferences;
- open questions or hypotheses.

Use calibrated language. A result in one benchmark does not establish general validity; a repeated claim does not become consensus without independent support; absence from the retrieved corpus does not prove absence from the field.

Never invent or reconstruct precise metadata, quotations, numerical results, or study details from memory. Mark exact uncertainty and verify it before using it in a strong claim.

Treat institution attribution as bibliographic metadata. Verify the leading institution or team from the paper byline/affiliations, official proceedings, repository record, or authoritative project page. Do not infer an institution merely from a familiar author name, email domain in a secondary index, or later employer.

Record provenance for every quantitative statement and data-bearing visual:

- source location and extraction context;
- whether the value is reported, reanalyzed, derived, or illustrative;
- population, dataset, split, metric, uncertainty, and comparison conditions when relevant;
- any transformation performed for the manuscript.

Never style illustrative values as measurements. Label them inline and exclude them from empirical conclusions. Do not claim replications, multiple runs, statistical significance, or aggregate performance unless the underlying evidence supports those descriptions.

## 2. Reading Depth

Start broad, then deepen selectively.

Use titles, abstracts, and metadata to place candidates provisionally. Read full methods, results, evaluation, and discussion for sources that:

- define the field or its vocabulary;
- control the taxonomy or central thesis;
- represent a major family;
- report a turning point, contradiction, or negative result;
- supply a benchmark, dataset, or metric used in a central comparison;
- support a claim whose fairness or scope is disputed.

Do not give every source equal space. Allocate depth according to its role in the survey argument.

## 3. Paper-Note Contract

Give each important source a dedicated note with the fields that apply:

- canonical citation and access path;
- verified affiliations or leading-team attribution, its exact evidence surface, and the authoritative surfaces checked if still unresolved;
- problem formulation and setting;
- main objects, assumptions, or population;
- mechanism, method, theory, or study design;
- data, benchmark, corpus, or evidence base;
- evaluation target and relevant result;
- authors' stated contribution;
- authors' stated limitations;
- survey-inferred strengths and limitations;
- relation to earlier and later work;
- role in this survey;
- unresolved verification needs, distinguishing source unavailability from local parsing or conversion failure.

Write `none identified yet` with a reason when no survey-inferred limitation is visible. Do not silently copy the paper's own limitation section as the review's complete judgment.

## 4. Evidence Units

Convert notes into evidence units before drafting. Record:

- **subject**: the paper, family, dataset, metric, theory, community, or phenomenon;
- **trace**: a verified result, design choice, quotation, data property, benchmark rule, or reproducibility fact;
- **relationship**: agreement, extension, contradiction, transfer, narrowing, dependency, or boundary;
- **movement**: what the unit changes in the field-level interpretation;
- **survey role**: why it belongs in this section;
- **strength and boundary**: what inference the evidence permits and what it does not.

An evidence unit earns manuscript space only when it moves an argument. A source name with no relationship or consequence remains a ledger item, not prose.

## 5. Three Levels Of Treatment

Treat important work at the level needed by the paragraph.

### Level 1: Factual introduction

State the problem, setting, core move, relevant evidence, and trajectory role. Omit procedural detail that does not affect interpretation.

### Level 2: Research evaluation

Explain what became possible, what evidence supports the claim, which assumptions constrain it, whether comparisons are fair, and what later evidence changes its credibility.

### Level 3: Survey-level critique

Judge how the work changed the field: foundation, turning point, bridge, representative case, contradiction, benchmark setter, dead end, or cautionary example. Ask whether it solved the stated problem, shifted the problem, or optimized a proxy; identify inherited assumptions and later confirmation, narrowing, or reversal.

Use Level 1 for background placement, Levels 1–2 for representative families, and all three levels for work that shapes the thesis, taxonomy, controversy, or gap map.

## 6. Paragraph Synthesis

Build a synthesis paragraph from:

1. a field-level claim;
2. two or more evidence units when comparison is needed;
3. the relationship among sources;
4. the survey's inference;
5. the boundary or unresolved consequence.

Allow a single source to anchor a paragraph only when it is genuinely foundational, uniquely evidentiary, or the object of focused critique. Otherwise, write from relationships rather than serial summaries.

Reject paragraphs that remain meaningful after arbitrary reordering of paper summaries. Reject citation piles whose sources do not have distinct roles. Integrate critique at the point where a comparison makes the limitation visible.

Do not force evidence into a prewritten thesis. Let contradictions, failed transfer, missing controls, alternative definitions, and benchmark mismatch revise the thesis and organization.

## 7. Consensus, Controversy, And Gaps

Track separately:

- **consensus**: claims supported across independent lines of evidence;
- **controversy**: disagreement traceable to definitions, assumptions, data, metrics, methods, or interpretation;
- **missing evidence**: influential claims with weak or indirect tests;
- **field drift**: proxies, datasets, or leaderboards replacing the original question;
- **survey inference**: a conclusion supported by the corpus but not stated by one source;
- **research gap**: an unresolved boundary whose importance and required evidence have been established.

Tie future directions to these demonstrated gaps. State what evidence would close a gap, not merely that more research is needed.

Audit source use at the end. Every included source should have a visible role in the evidence base, and every manuscript citation should support a nearby claim. Resolve unused sources by integration, reclassification, or removal; resolve unsupported prose by adding evidence, narrowing the claim, or deleting only the unneeded claim.
